Introduction

Artificial Intelligence, or AI, is a technology that equips machines, applications, and devices to simulate "human learning, comprehension, problem solving, decision making, creativity and autonomy" (Stryker & Kavlakoglu, 2026). AI has become increasingly prominent in everyday life, from automating redundant tasks to producing massive amounts of human-like content. As a result, AI has transformed how individuals receive and process information, enhancing productivity and improving key processes. It powers much of the technology behind digital assistants, chatbots, search engines, recommendation algorithms, and fraud prevention. However, with these benefits come pitfalls, such as data privacy issues, environmental concerns, plagiarism, cheating, and the inclusion of bias and discrimination.

Ethical Concerns and Policy Implementations

Plagiarism and Cheating

One major feature of AI, particularly generative AI, is its ability to produce content, such as images and videos, based on training data. Many individuals, however, argue that this content constitutes plagiarism because it infringes on the "intellectual property rights" of creators, raising an ethical issue surrounding ownership (Furze, 2023). Along with plagiarism, generative AI introduces issues of "fake news" or "deliberately harmful media." Usually delivered in the form of deepfakes, artificially produced news articles or videos can easily trick users into believing events that never occurred. Similarly, in academic settings, students use AI to generate essays, obtain answers, or complete other homework assignments without disclosing its usage. In both cases, the issue arises when users conceal explicit AI usage in content generation.

In terms of policy implementation, websites, news sites, and educators should shift from completely banning AI to encouraging users to state precisely how AI was used in content generation. Solutions include adding a watermark to AI-generated content, such as in artworks, videos, and images, and in academic settings, using the AIAS scale, a structured approach specifying "the extent of allowed GenAI use" for assignments, to moderate the level of AI usage by students in the creation of content (Perkins et al., 2024).

Data Privacy

Much of the data used to feed AI comes from users' online activities, and, like a self-feeding loop, AI processes this data to personalize recommendations or send targeted ads back to users. Most of the time, however, users are unaware of being tracked, raising concerns about data privacy, breaches, and the use of personal data for commercial gain. Location data, health data, shopping habits, likes, clicks, and views are some of the data points used by machine learning algorithms (Furze, 2023). Furthermore, companies sometimes automatically opt users into an agreement that allows them to use their data to train generative AI models, often without their consent. In some scenarios, LLMs, like ChatGPT, have unintentionally leaked user information in other conversations (Gomstyn & Jonker, 2026).

In terms of policy implementation, organizations should keep their data siloed to prevent AI from learning unnecessary sensitive information and accidentally leaking it in their output. Additionally, when users use an online service, companies should allow users to opt out of data collection or tracking. SharePoint.

Bias and Discrimination

Although AI has access to a vast wealth of data, some of this data is biased and perpetuates discriminatory ideas. For example, an AI trained to identify "terrorist behavior" may mark marginalized people as "terrorists" due to certain facial traits originating from subjective training data (Research Trends, 2023). Bias can endanger patients' lives in healthcare, increase misinformation in news sources or blogs, and stir hostility towards minority groups. Since the internet is increasingly being populated with AI-generated content, ignoring bias in current and future AI-generated content can further exacerbate these stereotypes.

In terms of policy implementations, governments and institutions should begin enforcing policies that require developers and engineers to test against diverse datasets to ensure they don't produce disparate outcomes for certain communities and types of people. Individual websites, especially social media sites and news sites, should focus on limiting or moderating partially or fully AI-generated content to remove possible discriminatory messages.

Environmental Concerns

AI uses a massive amount of energy. AI computing is carried out in the cloud, which relies on data centers and infrastructures (Furze, 2023). As AI usage rises, so does energy output; according to a study done by MIT, latest reports show that 4.4% of all the energy in the US now goes toward data centers, and that companies such as Apple, OpenAI, and Google, plan to spend billions on building additional data center in the coming years; between 2024 and 2028, energy usage going to data centers may triple to 12% (O'Donnell, 2025). As climate change is an emerging concern, the impact of AI may worsen its effects.

Implementing policies that encourage procedures for measuring the energy use and environmental impact of AI can encourage companies to disclose the environmental consequences of AI products. Additionally, tech companies can contribute by making AI algorithms more efficient, reducing overall demand for energy (UN environment programme, 2024).

Responsible AI Usage

As a data scientist, AI algorithms will have a profound impact on my life, both in how I use them for work and how I build them. Not only will I have to demonstrate responsible usage, such as fact-checking its responses to prevent over-reliance, but I will also, if I am building algorithms into an AI, have to review, clean, and organize training data to avoid bias. I must be able to explain why my AI made certain predictions and the larger consequences these predictions will have on those who interact with the algorithm. Being in the information field, it is my responsibility to exercise caution and ensure I understand the implications, such as environmental impact, of AI.

On a smaller scale, individuals can minimize the negative impacts of AI in many ways, such as limiting AI usage when necessary, double-checking AI output before sharing or publishing, limiting AI for political or racial research, and limiting the amount of private data shared in LLM chats. On a larger scale, individuals can advocate for policy changes, such as opting for green-friendly architecture for data centers or regulating training sets in AI.

Conclusion

Within the next ten to twenty years, AI will have a radical impact on everyday life. Privacy, environmental concerns, data collection, plagiarism, and cheating will become more prominent concerns as AI advances, and countries will need to work with global AI companies to ensure these technologies are used ethically and efficiently. Individuals can begin taking small steps to moderate their own AI usage by double-checking AI output and limiting usage for small tasks.
