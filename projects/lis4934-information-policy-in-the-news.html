USF LIS4934: Information Policy/Technology in the News

Published: 4/5/2026

Internet platforms serve as open communication hubs where users can share a wide spectrum of opinions and beliefs. Because the internet is inherently unregulated, hate content is commonplace. According to Castaño-Pulgarín et al. (2021), 80% of people have encountered hate speech online, and 40% have felt attacked or threatened via Social Network Sites. Such findings have prompted Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez, in collaboration with the Spanish Observatory on Racism and Xenophobia (OBERAXE), to design HODIO: an AI system which will "analyze large volumes of publicly available activity on social media to measure the scale and spread of online hate." OBERAXE plans to release a semi-annual report ranking social media platforms by prevalence of hate speech and introduce a legal offense for “algorithmic amplification” of hate (Observatorio Español del Racismo y la Xenofobia, n.d.; Wälde, 2026).

As Artificial Intelligence (AI) automation becomes a more common practice for content moderation, it is crucial to define (1) how AI algorithms determine the classification of hate speech, (2) how human bias, in conjunction with algorithmic output, is involved in the classification of hate speech, (3) how current U.S. legislation aligns with the algorithmic classification of online text and its relation to the design of HODIO, and (4) how algorithmic classification impacts information professionals and everyday users. By defining these four key topics, information professionals can understand the role algorithmic tools play in online hate classification, and users can understand how their online behaviors may impact these tools.

Online hate is defined as targeted verbal attacks, such as harassment, doxing, or degradation, based on an individual's race, sex, religion, immigration status, gender, sexual orientation, ethnicity, political orientation, or other characteristics (Walther, 2022). Social media companies often moderate content using a combination of manual dissemination and algorithmic classification. Manual dissemination is the removal of harmful content by human review, and algorithmic classification is the removal of harmful content by AI algorithmic tools. When users upload content, an AI tool scans it for harmful keywords, texts, images, or symbols, filters it, and then passes it on to a human moderator for final evaluation.

Unlike reviewing other types of content, such as copyright infringement, discerning hate from non-hate is challenging, as the language used in offensive remarks, such as those thrown toward religions or minority groups, is nuanced and highly context-specific. For example, in one study, researchers found that words in the African American English (AAE) dialect were "twice as likely to be labelled offensive compared to others," even when they weren't offensive in the specific circumstances (Sap et al., 2019). Algorithmic tools may fail to grasp the wider context and may censor items, such as religious or LGBTQ-related content, based on outdated models (New America, n.d.). Similarly, human reviewers who review flagged content may imbue their own personal judgment and bias. When posts are manually labeled as hate, algorithms remember and use this new information to further classify content, leading to a recursive feedback loop: the more a human reviewer identifies certain content as hateful, the more it enforces the "correctness" of the AI's response, and when a human moderator is under pressure to review thousands of posts every day, it makes it easier for both the AI and human moderator to reinforce such harmful biases.

To define hate speech, the United States has established certain laws and guidelines. Under the First Amendment, all speech, including that classified as "hate speech," is protected; speech is only considered illegal if it poses a true threat or incitement to lawless action or harassment. Although the government itself does not moderate hate speech, private companies do, according to Section 230: "service providers and users may not be held liable for voluntarily acting in good faith to restrict access to 'obscene, lewd, lascivious, filthy, excessively violent, harassing, or otherwise objectionable' material" (Section 230, 2026). In essence, the government's main responsibility is to ensure that companies are transparent about their definition of hate speech.

Similarly, the 2021 Filter Bubble Transparency Act, which was introduced in the House, aims to establish "requirements for large online platforms that use algorithms applying artificial intelligence or machine learning to user-specific data." (H.R.5921, 2022). If a platforms utilizes an "opaque algorithm", an "algorithmic ranking system that determines the order or manner that information is furnished to a user on a covered internet platform based," it must notify the user and make available a version of that platform that uses an "input-transform algorithm," which is an "algorithmic ranking system that does not use the user-specific data of a user to determine the order or manner that information is furnished to such user on a covered internet platform, unless the user-specific data is expressly provided to the platform by the user for such purpose." In the context of social media algorithmic processes, the Filter Bubble Transparency Act would encourage transparency between companies and users without instilling potential bias or judgment.

The implementation of HODIO would impact many individuals. Leaders of social media platforms may respond by adjusting how algorithmic tools classify hate speech; they may make them stricter, removing large amounts of polarizing content and potentially silencing non-hate speech. OBERAXE, a key stakeholder in this implementation, may become the gatekeeper of sentiment data in Spain. Critics will likely appear and act as legal challengers of the tool, arguing that public ranking of hate speech will encourage state-wide censorship. Vulnerable groups such as minors and marginalized individuals may benefit from HODIO, as restrictions may enforce harsher age verification for minors and reduce hate content targeted toward marginalized individuals. Overall, users may avoid engaging with controversial topics for fear of being silenced, skewing the representation of social media topics for researchers.

Information professionals, academic researchers, and data scientists will most likely take a critical and wary stance on HODIO's implementation. According to GPTZero (2025), two-thirds of Americans are unaware that they use products with AI features, and 74% believe that AI will spread misinformation. To combat these fears, information professionals will need to educate the public on how online interactions influence the effectiveness of algorithmic tools and ensure that algorithmic classification doesn't accidentally silence minority perspectives.

The implementation of HODIO will have global implications for online content moderation. If human moderators and data scientists neglect algorithmic tools, they may unintentionally allow biased judgment and the silencing of non-hate content; human moderators will need to ensure they don't instill their own biases within the review process. As the use of artificial intelligence increases in everyday business processes, regulations must ensure fair representation of key social media topics, which may be achieved through the 2021 Filter Bubble Transparency Act.

Artificial Intelligence is a powerful tool. If used wisely, it can speed up business processes and enhance the safety on online platforms; however, it can introduce bias and unfair judgment. Therefore, information professionals must diligently monitor and regulate this technology before nationwide deployment.
